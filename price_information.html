Make your case

Claim: What should we choose? State your ticket price.

Evidence: What information supports that choice? Give both average results for your choice and at least one other price.

Reasoning: Why does the evidence make this a good choice? Explain your minimum and why the balance of profit and completion is worth choosing.

Compare prices that meet your minimum. Among those, look for higher average profit.

Teacher note: how to use the information

The worksheet provides results for $10, $20, and $50. Students use the simulator to fill the five remaining rows before comparing prices. Keep the full teacher-reference table closed during student trials; it is excluded from the worksheet printout. Students choose and justify their own minimum, then compare eligible prices. If none meets their minimum, they can report that or explain a revised standard and check all their comparisons again.

Average profit is the mean of 1,000 nightly profits. Completion combines all admitted guests across those nights: total who finish both houses ÷ total admitted. Each guest has equal weight. The “out of 100” explanation describes the proportion, not a literal group of 100 or a promise that every night has the same result. No separate satisfaction score is used. The animation is one actual night chosen for profit closest to the average; its completion percentage may differ from the aggregate displayed here.

Rounding percentages down preserves accurate comparisons with whole-percent minimums. No option is automatically marked as the best. A recommendation depends on the group’s standard and the prices they compared.
